## Pre agentic SW Factories

This turned out to have **two genuinely distinct, well-documented historical senses** — not the single DevOps-pipeline guess in the task brief, but an older industrial-manufacturing sense (1960s–1980s) and a more recent, still pre-LLM, government/enterprise DevSecOps sense (2017–2022). Both explicitly use the phrase "software factory," both are strongly corroborated, and both are good material for a before/after framing since neither one involves AI writing code — humans write all the code in both.

### Sense 1: The 1960s–1980s industrial/manufacturing sense (Bemer → Japan)

- The term traces to **Robert W. Bemer**, an engineer and manager for systems and software engineering integration at General Electric, who proposed a "software factory" in his **1968 paper "The economics of program production"** as a response to the "software crisis" — standardized tools, a computer-based interface, and a historical database for financial/management control, aimed at reducing variability in programmer productivity. 🟡 secondary — corroborated across multiple independent write-ups ([Addy Osmani's own blog, which frames Bemer as envisioning software as "a repeatable and instrumentable production process" rather than individual craft, and notes the idea "generally fell flat" for decades](https://addyosmani.com/blog/software-factories/) 🟢 for the retelling itself being Osmani's own words; [TrueFoundry's history section, independently dating the same 1968 proposal](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/software-factory-agentic-enterprise-guide); search-engine-synthesized summaries of Bemer's own papers archive) — no primary Bemer text itself was directly fetched in this pass ([Computer History Museum's archive of Bemer's published papers](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2019/02/102785423-05-01-acc.pdf) exists but was not opened), so treat the exact paper title/date as secondary-corroborated rather than primary-verified.
- **Hitachi was the first company to actually adopt the "software factory" label**, establishing **Hitachi Software Works in 1969**. **System Development Corporation (SDC)** followed in 1975, then **NEC, Toshiba, and Fujitsu in 1976–1977** — 🟡 secondary, consistent across the [IEEE Annals of the History of Computing paper "Factory Concepts and Practices in Software Development"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1109/MAHC.1991.10004) and search-synthesized summaries of it.
- Through the 1980s these Japanese firms — Hitachi, Toshiba, NEC, Fujitsu, and NTT — ran facilities that were literally called (and functioned as) software factories, applying manufacturing-style discipline: code reuse, standardized design/testing/reuse support tools, and project-management support, explicitly aimed at "creating software that was not inferior in performance and superior in price through industrial standardization." 🟡 secondary, corroborated across the [IEEE Annals paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1109/MAHC.1991.10004) and an independent Japanese-language retrospective, [「ソフトウェア工場」は40年前に作られた ("'Software Factory' was created 40 years ago")](https://note.com/takuya_kubo_1986/n/n0494501221ad?hl=en).
- Concrete, named, primary-flavored figures: at **Toshiba's Fuchu Software Factory**, code reuse rose from **13% in 1979 to 48% in 1985**, and productivity grew from **1,390 lines per person-month in 1976 to over 3,100 lines per person-month in 1985**. 🟡 secondary — same two sources above agree on these specific numbers, which is good independent corroboration for a single specific figure-set.
- The definitive academic treatment is **Michael A. Cusumano's 1991 book, *Japan's Software Factories: A Challenge to U.S. Management*** (Oxford University Press) — devotes chapters to working methods at SDC, Hitachi, Toshiba, NEC, and Fujitsu, and explicitly frames the phenomenon as large numbers of engineers developing software cooperatively, with individual programs built from (and yielding) reusable components rather than developed in isolation. 🟡 secondary — corroborated across the [Oxford University Press catalog page](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/japans-software-factories-9780195062168), [ACM Digital Library's guide-book entry](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/119989), and independent booksellers; not directly fetched/read in full in this pass, so treat as bibliographically confirmed but not text-verified.
- A second, distinct **1990s–2000s echo of the term with an unrelated meaning** is worth flagging so the chapter doesn't conflate it with the industrial-Japan sense: **Jack Greenfield and Keith Short's 2004 book *Software Factories: Assembling Applications with Patterns, Models, Frameworks, and Tools*** (Wiley) used "software factory" for a **model-driven development** approach — visual/domain-specific-language-driven assembly of framework-based components, an alternative to OMG's Model Driven Architecture that didn't use UML. Greenfield and Short were architects in Microsoft's Enterprise Frameworks and Tools group. 🟡 secondary, corroborated across multiple bookseller/publisher listings ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Software_Factories.html?id=06dQAAAAMAAJ), [the book's own companion site](https://www.softwarefactories.com/TheBook.html)) — not fetched/read in full, and given only for completeness since this sense is not the one that connects to the chapter's agentic-factory framing; the chapter should probably skip it or mention it only in passing.

### Sense 2: The 2017–2022 DoD/DevSecOps sense — a stronger candidate for the chapter's "before" picture

This turned out to be the single best-documented, most concretely-named pre-agentic usage found, and it is directly a **CI/CD/DevSecOps automation pipeline** (matching the second guess in the task brief) rather than the industrial-Japan sense — but it's a specific, named, dated government program, not generic "DevOps."

- The U.S. Department of Defense/Air Force stood up **Kessel Run**, its first "software factory," in **April 2017** at Hanscom Air Force Base and in Boston — founded by Col. Enrique Oti, Capt. Kroger, and Lt. Col. Jeremiah Sanders out of the Air Force Air Operations Center program office, explicitly to bring commercial-tech-industry-style coding speed to military software after the Air Force scrapped an over-budget, behind-schedule contractor-built system. Named after Han Solo's "Kessel Run" from Star Wars. 🟡 secondary, corroborated across [Air & Space Forces Magazine's retrospective](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/making-the-kessel-run/), [Wikipedia's Kessel Run entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kessel_Run), and search-synthesized coverage; grew from a small team to roughly **two thousand airmen, government civilians, and contractors within three years**.
- **Platform One**, the DoD's central DevSecOps enterprise-services team, was established in **December 2019** by the Air Force to provide the underlying toolchain (CI/CD, hardened containers, continuous authority-to-operate) so that individual mission teams didn't each have to build their own pipeline; it manages/supports several other named factories (**Kobayashi Maru, Space CAMP, Unified Platform**). 🟡 secondary, corroborated across [FedTech Magazine's "How Software Factories Help the DOD Scale DevSecOps"](https://fedtechmagazine.com/article/2022/04/how-software-factories-help-dod-scale-devsecops-perfcon) and independent search results.
- By **2022** the DoD operated (or owned) **29 software factories** across services, including Kessel Run, Cloud One (Air Force cloud), Black Pearl (Navy Platform-as-a-Service with DevSecOps built in), Kobayashi Maru, Space CAMP, and Unified Platform; a February 2022 DoD strategy explicitly aimed to "democratize exceptionalism" — i.e., replicate the Kessel Run model DoD-wide rather than leave it as an isolated pocket of excellence — with a 180-day implementation-plan requirement. 🟡 secondary, [FedTech Magazine, quoting DoD's Danielle Metz and Red Hat's Jason Anderson](https://fedtechmagazine.com/article/2022/04/how-software-factories-help-dod-scale-devsecops-perfcon).
- **A working definition of this pre-agentic sense, drawn from these sources**: a software factory here means developers, users, and management working together daily inside one environment; **automated build and test** that surfaces issues quickly; multitenancy so many teams share one pipeline; and (ideally) a **continuous authorization-to-operate** posture — i.e., the pipeline (build/test/deploy/security-compliance) is industrialized and automated, but a human still writes every line of application code. This is explicitly framed by practitioners as "more than just automation" in the sense of caring about usable, valuable software, not merely fast pipelines — but it is still humans-write-code automation, not agentic code generation. 🟡 secondary, same FedTech source.
- A **currently-active community structure** for this sense: the [Software Factory Coalition](https://coalition.dso.mil/), bringing together 11 DoD software-factory organizations (Kessel Run, Corsair Ranch, LevelUP/Platform One, Army Software Factory, Bespin, Kobayashi Maru, Overmatch Software Armory, Space CAMP, CDAO, and others) to share practices and reduce duplication — confirms this is a real, named, multi-organization ecosystem rather than a one-off program. 🟢 primary (the coalition's own site), though it gives no founding date or history of its own.
- **A notable, well-dated bridge piece directly connecting this pre-agentic sense to the agentic one**: "[The End of the Assembly Line: Why the DoD Software Factory Era Is Over](https://www.averagegeniuses.com/the-end-of-the-assembly-line-why-the-dod-software-factory-era-is-over/)" (published **December 16, 2025**) argues the DoD-factory model (Kessel Run, Platform One) is being outpaced by commercial AI-native platforms (naming Palantir, Anduril, Scale AI) and low-code/agentic tooling — its citable framing: *"a junior officer with a low-code platform or a generative AI agent stack can build that same application in an afternoon,"* and the summary line **"Software Factories didn't fail; they were outpaced."** Kessel Run itself reportedly pivoted toward a vendor-led model and Platform One toward integration-architecture rather than in-house code development. 🟠 single-source — one commentary piece, not independently corroborated elsewhere in this pass, but useful as an explicit, dated "old model gives way to new model" quote that names the transition directly.

### Assessment

**Strongly corroborated overall.** Both senses (1960s–80s industrial/Japan, and 2017–2022 DoD DevSecOps) are independently confirmed across multiple non-affiliated sources (an IEEE academic paper, a Japanese-language retrospective, Cusumano's own published book, and — separately — FedTech Magazine, Air & Space Forces Magazine, Wikipedia, and the DoD coalition's own site). The specific Toshiba Fuchu numbers and the Kessel Run/Platform One founding details are the most concretely citable facts. The weakest link is that no primary Bemer 1968 text or Cusumano 1991 book text was directly read in this pass — those are bibliographically solid but not text-verified, so treat exact wording/quotes from them as unconfirmed even though their existence and content-summary are well corroborated.

## Agentic SW Factories

Industry writers converge on a fairly consistent definition of what makes a factory specifically **"agentic"** — the throughline across every source below is the same one already established in `notes/initial-notes/Software Factory.md`'s Harness → Loop → Factory framing (Addy Osmani), but this pass finds several *independent* sources arriving at compatible definitions from different angles (a consultancy, a vendor, an infra-tooling company), which strengthens rather than merely repeats that prior finding.

### The core distinction: agentic = agents do the SDLC work, not just move it faster

- **Addy Osmani's own definition** (directly fetched, 🟢 primary): the factory is "**many harnessed loops running at once, fed by a queue of work and drained through a review gate into production, with humans owning the whole thing from above**"; the paradigm shift is "**from writing code to building and running the factory that writes it**," where "the unit of work shifts up a level, to the loop, the harness, and the flow between them, rather than the individual code diff." Critically: "a software factory represents **not a bigger agent; it is an org chart made of loops**." [addyosmani.com/blog/software-factories](https://addyosmani.com/blog/software-factories/) — this both confirms and sharpens the framing already captured in the initial notes file.
- **Factory.ai's own "Factory 2.0" manifesto** (published **June 2026**, 🟢 primary, directly fetched): explicitly contrasts itself against pipelines that merely "speed up coding" — the key line is that traditional DevOps pipelines are "**static, manually-triggered sequences**," whereas an agentic software factory is a "**continuous feedback loop**" where signals (bug reports, customer feedback, requirements) get triaged, planned, built, tested, reviewed, secured, shipped, and monitored, with monitoring itself generating new signals that re-enter the loop — "**This system will be built by engineers, and in turn will build their software.**" Three named pillars: **model independence** (routing across multiple models rather than one), **sovereign intelligence** (the system "learns from itself, feeding every agent session, code review, and resolved incident back into the loop," staying inside the org's own infrastructure), and **continual learning** (different SDLC stages sharing "the same agent core" so a security finding informs code review, a deployment triggers documentation, etc). [factory.ai/news/software-factory](https://factory.ai/news/software-factory).
- **TrueFoundry's explicit historical bridge** (🟡 secondary, directly fetched): frames the agentic factory as "**a repeatable delivery system — standardized inputs, a defined assembly path, automated quality control, repeatable outputs**" where autonomous AI agents now perform planning, implementation, testing, and review, while engineers define intent and review results — explicitly stating "**traditional factories standardized human workflows. Agentic factories introduce autonomous execution**." Their history section runs 1968 (Bemer) → 1975 (SDC) → 2004 (Greenfield/Short's book) → 2010s (enterprise CI/CD platforms, citing **BMW's platform serving 12,000+ developers running ~140,000 daily CI jobs**) → 2026 (agent-native reinterpretation) — i.e., an independent write-up drawing the exact same "pre-agentic vs agentic" line this chapter needs, citing a concrete large-scale pre-agentic CI/CD example (BMW) that is new to this research pass. [truefoundry.com/blog/software-factory-agentic-enterprise-guide](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/software-factory-agentic-enterprise-guide).
- **BCG Platinion's framing** (🟡 secondary, directly fetched, a consultancy rather than a tool vendor — useful as a non-vendor-interested source): "**Autonomous AI agents build, test, and ship software solutions around the clock, while humans define business intent and review outcomes**" — explicitly contrasts this with 2022-era AI copilots, which delivered only "incremental gains" (cited up to ~30% productivity improvement) while still requiring humans to write and review every line. Frames the two decisive human competencies in the agentic-factory era as **"harness engineering"** (designing/refining the factory's architecture) and **"intent thinking"** (translating business needs into precise, testable descriptions of desired outcomes) — this "intent thinking" framing is a distinct, citable addition not found in the other sources. Cites named figures: **3–5x average productivity gains** (practitioner estimate), **OpenAI building roughly a million lines of code in an internal beta with three engineers steering agents over five months / zero manually written code**, and **Spotify cutting a studied migration's time by 60–90%, merging 1,500+ agent-generated PRs** (with a further claim that Spotify engineers "haven't written code since December 2025" for that workflow — a striking, specific claim that should be flagged as vendor/consultancy-sourced and unverified elsewhere in this pass). [bcgplatinion.com/insights/the-agentic-software-factory](https://www.bcgplatinion.com/insights/the-agentic-software-factory).
- **A useful negative/skeptical corroboration for the same line**: the same DoD bridge piece cited above ("[The End of the Assembly Line](https://www.averagegeniuses.com/the-end-of-the-assembly-line-why-the-dod-software-factory-era-is-over/)") independently draws the identical distinction from the opposite direction — arguing the *old* (pre-agentic, pipeline-only) factory model is obsolete specifically because it only sped up a human-driven process rather than having agents do the work — reinforcing that "humans write code, pipeline just moves it faster" vs. "agents do the SDLC work" is the operative line industry-wide, not just marketing language from AI vendors. 🟠 single-source for this specific piece, but the underlying distinction it draws matches the vendor/consultancy sources above.

### Convergent definition across all sources

Every source in this section — spanning a solo practitioner blog (Osmani), an agentic-coding vendor (Factory.ai), an AI-infra vendor (TrueFoundry), and a management consultancy (BCG Platinion) — draws the "agentic" line at the same place: **the presence or absence of autonomous execution of the actual SDLC work (planning, coding, testing, reviewing) by AI agents**, as opposed to automation that merely standardizes or accelerates a pipeline that humans still execute by hand. None of these sources treat "agentic" as simply "has more automation" or "uses AI somewhere" — the bar specifically is agents doing multi-step, tool-using work across the SDLC with humans repositioned to define intent and review outcomes, not write code. This is a genuinely convergent, multi-source definition, not a single vendor's marketing framing restated four times.

### Assessment

**Strongly corroborated for the core definition** (four independent, differently-motivated sources converge on the same "autonomous SDLC execution vs. pipeline automation" line), but **individual named-company metrics are vendor/consultancy-sourced and should be flagged as such if used** — the Spotify "haven't written code since December 2025" and OpenAI "million lines, three engineers, zero manual code" claims in particular are single-source-per-claim (BCG Platinion's own reporting) and not independently audited or corroborated elsewhere in this research pass; treat them the same way the prior note treated Factory.ai's own customer metrics (real, named, specific, but vendor-reported).
